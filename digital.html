Language Preservation & Revitalization

Tribal libraries and tribal college libraries serve as language revitalization centers. The suppression and attempted erasure of many Indigenous languages is tied to settler colonial assimilation practices, including the boarding-school era. Through collection development, programming, and digital archives, tribal libraries support Indigenous language revitalization.

Key Indigenous languages served by tribal libraries: Cherokee, Navajo/Diné, Lakota, Ojibwe, Seneca (Onöndowaʼga꞉ʼ), Passamaquoddy, Karuk, Hawaiian, Inupiaq, Dakota, Assiniboine, Cree, Mohawk

Funding Challenges

Tribal libraries have been historically underfunded compared to mainstream public libraries. IMLS Basic Grants (~$6,000-$10,000) are small relative to the operating budgets of typical public libraries, and Enhancement Grants (up to $200,000) are competitive and not guaranteed. ALA and ATALM have both advocated for sustained and increased federal investment in tribal library services.

Key Technology Facts

  • U.S. public libraries provided ~260,000 public-access internet computers and recorded ~100.9 million public-internet-user sessions in FY2024 (IMLS Public Libraries Survey).
  • U.S. public libraries recorded ~418 million Wi-Fi sessions in FY2024 (IMLS PLS field WIRESRU); combined with public-computer sessions, libraries hosted ~519 million in-library internet uses in a single year.
  • Free public Wi-Fi is near-universal at U.S. public libraries: ALA reported 98% of public libraries offered free Wi-Fi in 2015, up from 91% in 2012 (Wikipedia: Digital divide in the United States; Wikipedia: Public library).
  • The E-rate program (USAC/FCC, authorized by the 1996 Telecommunications Act) provides 20-90% discounts on telecom, internet, and internal connections for libraries and schools; the total program budget is ~$3.9B/year (raised from $2.4B in 2014).
  • Library-attributed E-rate commitments totaled ~$2.28 billion over FY2016-FY2026 (~$235M/year average), with fiber accounting for 64% of library E-rate spending — most libraries now have fiber or high-speed connections (this project's USAC Form 471 analysis).
  • ALA reports 24 million U.S. households still lack home internet, making libraries essential to closing the digital divide (ALA Broadband page).
  • Pew Research (2013, 6,224 Americans 16+) found 56% of internet users without home access say public libraries' basic technological resources (computers, internet, printers) are 'very important' to them and their family, vs 33% of all respondents; 77% of those without home access say library tech is important overall.
  • Approximately 23% of Americans lack a home broadband connection and 7% (~23 million people) do not use the internet at all; public libraries are the primary no-fee internet access point for these populations (Wikipedia: Digital divide in the United States).
  • The Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation's U.S. Libraries Program (1997-2018) connected public libraries to the internet across all 50 states; in 2003 alone it contributed $250 million to install 47,000+ computers and train librarians in ~11,000 libraries. The foundation committed more than $1 billion cumulatively to library programs before winding down Global Libraries in 2018.
  • The first public library makerspace opened at Fayetteville Free Library (New York) in 2011; library makerspaces typically offer 3D printers, laser cutters, 3D scanners, coding/robotics kits, and audio/video tools (Wikipedia: Library makerspace).
  • Library hotspot lending grew rapidly 2013-2023; the 2023 FCC Learn Without Limits initiative proposed permanent E-rate eligibility for off-premises Wi-Fi hotspots, building on 'a decade of library hotspot and device lending expertise' (ALA State of America's Libraries 2024).
  • As of 2012, 90% of public libraries offered formal or informal technology training; for many communities the library is the only agency offering free computer classes and digital-skills learning (Wikipedia: Public library).
  • In October 2025 the FCC voted 2-1 to end E-rate support for Wi-Fi hotspots and school-bus Wi-Fi, prompting ALA and coalition partners to launch a 'Save Our E-rate' campaign (Wikipedia: E-Rate; ALA Broadband page).
  • In 2006, 73% of library branches reported being the only local provider of free public computer and internet access; a 2008 study found 100% of rural, high-poverty library outlets provided public internet access (Wikipedia: Public library).
  • 98% of US public libraries offer free public WiFi, making them the primary free internet access point in many communities (ALA Digital Inclusion Survey 2014).

Key Findings

  • U.S. public libraries held ~731.8M physical collection items in FY2024 (639M books, 31M audio, 62M video) across 9,249 reporting systems serving 343M people (IMLS PLS, this project). Academic libraries added ~705.5M physical book volumes and ~1.47B e-book holdings in 2022-23 (IPEDS AL, this project).
  • Estimated total U.S. library book volumes held exceed 1.3 billion (public + academic), before counting school, special, and government-document collections; adding e-books and electronic serial titles pushes total collection items well over 5 billion.
  • Total public library circulation was ~1.71B items in FY2024 (4.97 per capita), down ~22% from the FY2019 baseline of 2.19B (6.71 per capita) after the COVID-19 collapse and partial recovery. Circulation peaked around FY2008-FY2010 at ~2.3-2.4B and has declined gradually as digital formats and search displaced physical borrowing.
  • Digital/electronic circulation reached ~554.8M in FY2024 (32.5% of total), comprising ~316M e-book, ~245M e-audio, and ~21M e-video circulations (IMLS PLS, this project). OverDrive's global platform checkouts grew from ~331M (2019) to ~555M (2022), illustrating the structural shift to digital lending.
  • Audiobooks are the fastest-growing trade format: 91,000 titles released in 2023 (up from ~46,000 in 2018), and 2022 was the first year audiobook revenue surpassed e-book revenue (Audio Publishers Association).
  • Print remains the dominant format -- 64% of American readers read print books in 2025 vs. 31% e-books and 26% audiobooks -- but print's share fell 8 points (from 72%) while e-books more than doubled and audiobooks more than tripled over 2011-2025 (Pew/Gallup via this project).
  • Public library electronic-materials expenditure rose 61.1% from FY2019 ($449M) to FY2024 ($724M) (IMLS PLS, this project), while total estimated materials spending across all U.S. library sectors is ~$5.6B/yr (public ~$1.8B, academic ~$3.3B, school ~$0.5B).
  • Academic library materials spending ($3.23B in 2022-23) is dominated by serials/database 'big deal' packages (78.9% of materials), not monographs; 98.5% of academic serial titles are now electronic (IPEDS AL, this project).

Key Findings

  • ILS market is consolidating under three power-centers: Ex Libris/Clarivate (Alma+Primo + Sierra+Polaris), OCLC (WMS + Tipasa + resource sharing), and open-source (Koha/Evergreen/FOLIO, often supported by ByWater & Equinox). SirsiDynix Symphony remains the single largest public-library ILS by install count.
  • Open-source ILS is the growth segment: a 2013 survey put open-source ILS at ~14% of industry activity; by the mid-2020s Koha (the most widely implemented open-source ILS worldwide) plus Evergreen (~1,800 libraries globally) plus FOLIO together represent the principal counter-trend to vendor consolidation.
  • Discovery is a two-vendor academic race (Primo vs EDS) plus OCLC WorldCat Discovery and SirsiDynix e-Library dominating their host-ILS footprints; open-source VuFind and Aspen Discovery are notable replacements.
  • E-book lending is overwhelmingly OverDrive/Libby: 600M digital checkouts in 2023 across ~92,000 libraries & schools in ~100 countries. Hoopla is the strong #2 (2,700+ library partners, simultaneous-use model). cloudLibrary (Bibliotheca) and the sunsetted RBdigital (absorbed by OverDrive 2020-21) round out the field.
  • The 3M library-systems divestiture (2015) is the defining event in RFID/self-check: Bibliotheca took North America + cloudLibrary; Nedap took the rest of world. ITG is the principal U.S.-headquartered competitor; Lyngsoe leads large-scale automated sorting.
  • Envisionware is the de-facto leader for PC-reservation + pay-for-print (LPT:One), an essential layer over ~260,000 public-access terminals and ~101M annual sessions (IMLS PLS FY2024).
  • Website/CMS has no single winner: WordPress/Drupal for general sites, Springshare LibGuides near-universal in academic, BiblioCommons for the largest urban public systems, and vendor-hosted catalogs doubling as websites for the smallest libraries.
  • OCLC is the library-technology utility layer: ~30,000 member libraries in 100+ countries, $217.8M revenue (FY2020-21), runs WorldCat (largest OPAC in the world), the largest resource-sharing network (10,000+ libraries, 3M+ e-resource requests/yr), and a broad product line spanning cataloging (Connexion), ILS/LSP (WMS), discovery (WorldCat Discovery), ILL (Tipasa/WorldShare ILL/ILLiad), and digital collections (CONTENTdm).

Key Findings

  • Interlibrary loan is a high-volume, deeply embedded service: U.S. public libraries alone processed ~137.3M ILL transactions in FY2024 (70.7M borrowed, 66.6M lent), the highest borrowed-year on record. ILL peaked at ~141.5M total in FY2013, crashed to 99.7M in FY2020 (COVID-19), and has rebounded to near pre-pandemic levels -- demonstrating both the resilience and the scale of resource sharing.
  • OCLC is the backbone of American resource sharing. Founded in 1967 as the Ohio College Library Center, it is now the largest library consortium with ~30,000 member libraries in 100+ countries, operates the world's largest resource-sharing network (10,000+ libraries), delivers 3M+ e-resource requests/year, and maintains WorldCat and the Dewey Decimal Classification. Its Express program fills copy requests in an average of 10 hours across 1,000+ participating libraries.
  • State funding is the lifeblood of statewide consortia but has eroded since the 2000s. VIVA (Virginia) saw its state General Assembly share fall from 77% (FY2006) to 44% (FY2012); Illinois consolidated nine regional library systems into two (RAILS and IHLS) in 2011 to preserve services amid state-aid cuts; Massachusetts merged six regional systems into the single Massachusetts Library System in 2010. The shift pushes consortia toward dues-funded and grant-funded models.
  • Academic consortia deliver enormous group-purchasing power. OhioLINK (88 institutions / 117 libraries), CARLI/I-Share (90+ Illinois academic libraries), PALCI (70+ Pennsylvania libraries), VIVA (74 Virginia institutions), and GALILEO (2,000+ Georgia institutions of all types) negotiate e-resource licenses that typically save members 30-70% versus individual negotiation. The largest, Lyrasis, serves 1,000+ members with ~$139M revenue.
  • Shared print programs are transforming collection stewardship. HathiTrust commits members to retaining ~18 million monograph volumes for 25 years; the Big Ten Academic Alliance (formerly CIC) coordinates distributed last-copy retention across 14 universities; WEST (100+ western libraries) and EAST (50+ eastern libraries) manage journal-run last-copy retention. These agreements let libraries deaccession duplicates while guaranteeing collective access.
  • High-density shared storage facilities hold the physical last copies. The Harvard Depository (founded 1986) pioneered modular compact storage; ReCAP -- jointly operated by Columbia, Princeton, and the New York Public Library in Plainsboro, NJ -- holds 12M+ volumes and expanded in 2017. The WRLC operates a shared facility for eight D.C.-area universities.
  • Statewide borrowing cards extend access far beyond a patron's home library. The TexShare Card (Texas, since 1999), Ohio's layered OPLIN/SearchOhio/OhioLINK access, Michigan's MeLCat (400+ libraries), and Connecticut's iCONN/ResearchITCT let residents borrow across hundreds of participating libraries. PALCI's E-ZBorrow and the Orbis Cascade Summit provide the academic equivalent for 70+ and 37 member libraries respectively.
  • Physical delivery is the hidden infrastructure of resource sharing. Illinois (IHLS + RAILS) and Massachusetts (MLS) run the highest-volume courier networks per library, moving ILL items among 1,800+ and hundreds of libraries respectively. Delivery is funded by state library agencies through state aid and LSTA; cuts to state aid directly reduce delivery frequency.

Key Findings

  • The U.S. library cataloging landscape is split between two classification schedules: Dewey (DDC) dominates public and school libraries (~70% of public libraries) while the Library of Congress Classification (LCC) dominates academic and research collections, reflecting each scheme's design intent.
  • DDC has had 23 print editions, with DDC 23 (2011) the final print edition; OCLC now maintains the system continuously online through WebDewey. The copyright and licensing remain with OCLC (which acquired Forest Press in 1988), unlike LCC, which LC maintains freely.
  • RDA replaced AACR2 as the cataloging code for the Anglo-American world; it was released in June 2010 and adopted by the Library of Congress and peer national libraries by March 31, 2013. RDA is built on the FRBR/FRAD (and now IFLA LRM) conceptual models.
  • MARC21 remains the dominant record-encoding format, but it is in a managed transition toward BIBFRAME, the Library of Congress's linked-data model (BIBFRAME 2.0, 2016). The two standards coexist, with MARC-to-BIBFRAME conversion tooling and the Sinopia editor enabling production linked-data cataloging.
  • OCLC and WorldCat form the backbone of U.S. cataloging cooperation. WorldCat contains more than 540 million bibliographic records representing over 2 billion holdings from roughly 16,000 member libraries in more than 100 countries; the majority of U.S. cataloging is copy cataloging sourced from WorldCat.
  • Authority control in the U.S. is anchored by LC's Name Authority File (LCNAF) and Subject Headings (LCSH, ~350,000+ headings). OCLC extends these through FAST (faceted LCSH, ~1.8M records) and VIAF (a ~30M-record international name-authority hub), all increasingly available as open linked data.
  • Subject access is highly discipline-specific: medical libraries use NLM's MeSH (~30,000 descriptors), education libraries use the ERIC Thesaurus (~11,000+ descriptors), art libraries use the Getty AAT (~70,000+ terms), and school/small public libraries use the Sears List - all crosswalked to LCSH.
  • A family of metadata schemas supplements MARC for digital and preservation work: Dublin Core (interoperability), MODS (library-style XML), METS (object packaging), PREMIS (preservation), VRA Core (visual resources), and EAD (archival finding aids), with CIDOC-CRM emerging as a shared cultural-heritage ontology.
