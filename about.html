About / Methodology

About this wiki

How this dataset was built, where the data comes from, and what's still missing.

Overview

This is a living, automated-crawl dataset of every public and private library in the United States, plus every federal, state, county, city, tribal, and special-district government website. Each record carries address, geocoordinates, website URL, funding information, contact details, review ratings, hours of operation, services offered, and area demographics. Every link is verified live, and the dataset is continuously enriched via a scheduled pipeline.

Known Gaps (honest accounting)

  • 14.5% of public library systems (1,339) have no website — almost all are tiny rural/bookmobile libraries with no online presence.
  • 54.1% of public libraries remain unrated — Google Places API daily quota limits accumulation; Gemini LLM fills in well-known libraries but correctly omits obscure branches.
  • 25.6% of gov sites are not live — many are genuinely expired/dead .gov domains (DNS won't resolve).
  • Special libraries (law, medical, theological, corporate) are underrepresented — only 75 added beyond the 3,695 academic libraries.
  • Cron automation is set up but not yet active — run bash automation/setup_cron.sh to enable continuous enrichment.

Update Frequency

The pipeline is designed to run every 30 minutes via cron (automation/run_pipeline.sh). Each run is idempotent and resumable — it picks up where the last run left off, using caches in data/_cache/. Google Places API quota resets daily at midnight PT, so ratings accumulate across runs. The wiki can be rebuilt at any time by re-running python3 wiki/build_wiki.py.

Key Historical Facts

  • The earliest American libraries were private collections of gentlemen, clergy, and colleges; Anglican parish libraries subsidized by the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel (founded 1701) appeared at colonial churches.
  • The Library Company of Philadelphia (1731, Benjamin Franklin and the Junto) was the first successful subscription library in America and hired Louis Timothee, the first librarian hired in America; it served virtually as the Library of Congress until the national capital was established in 1800.
  • The Darby Free Library (founded 1743 as the Darby Library Company by 29 Quakers) claims to be 'the oldest library in the United States in continuous service'; it was a subscription library until 1898 and celebrated its 275th anniversary in 2018.
  • The Redwood Library and Athenaeum (1747, Newport, RI) is the first purposely built library in the US and the oldest neo-Classical building in the country, in continuous use since its opening; it predates the Charleston Library Society (1748), New York Society Library (1754), and Boston Athenaeum (1807).
  • The Library of Congress was established April 24, 1800; British forces burned its original collection in August 1814; Congress bought Thomas Jefferson's 6,487 books for $23,950 in January 1815; and it gained copyright deposit rights in 1870.
  • Peterborough, New Hampshire, founded the first tax-supported free public library in the US (and the world) at town meeting on April 9, 1833; its success led New Hampshire to pass enabling legislation in 1849.
  • The Boston Public Library (statute 1848, established 1852, opened March 20, 1854) was the first large free municipal public library in the US; it is Massachusetts' Library for the Commonwealth and the third-largest public library in the US (~24 million items).
  • The American Library Association, founded October 6, 1876, in Philadelphia by 103 librarians (90 men, 13 women), is the oldest and largest library association in the world; Justin Winsor was its first president (1876-1885).
  • Melvil Dewey published the Dewey Decimal Classification in 1876 and opened the first library school in the world - the School of Library Economy at Columbia College - on January 5, 1887, with 20 students (17 women, admitted against the Regents' wishes).
  • Andrew Carnegie funded 2,509 libraries worldwide (1,689 in the US) between 1883 and 1929; $40 million built 1,670 public library buildings in 1,412 American communities, and by 1930 half of all US public libraries had been built by Carnegie.
  • Under segregation, Carnegie funded separate libraries for African Americans in the South (e.g., Houston Colored Carnegie Library; the Carnegie Library of Savannah, GA, opened 1914) rather than insisting on integration.
  • Women's clubs led the establishment of 75-80% of libraries in communities across the country during Carnegie's era, organizing local fundraising and lobbying.
  • The ALA Library War Service (1917-1920) raised $5 million, erected 36 camp libraries (with a $320,000 Carnegie Corporation grant), distributed 7-10 million books and magazines, and served 500+ locations including military hospitals in WWI; it led to library departments in the Army, Navy, Veterans' Association, and Merchant Marine, and the American Library in Paris (1918).
  • The first Library Bill of Rights was drafted by Forrest Spaulding and adopted by the ALA in 1939, defining modern librarianship's commitment to intellectual freedom; the ALA strengthened it in 1948 and amended it in 1961 to bar discrimination by race, religion, national origin, or political views.
  • The National Library Service for the Blind (Pratt-Smoot Act, began July 1, 1931) is the federal library service for blind and print-disabled Americans; it was amended to add sound recordings/talking books (1934), blind children (1952), music materials (1962), and physical impairments (1966).

    Key Findings

    • COVID-19 caused the sharpest one-year drop in US public library usage on record: visits fell 41.6% (FY2019 -> FY2020) and circulation fell 25.0%.
    • Despite the FY2020 trough, library income actually rose 2.1% in FY2020 thanks to pre-pandemic budgets and federal relief.
    • By FY2024, visits had recovered to 69.3% of baseline and programs to 85.8% -- meaningful but incomplete recovery.
    • Circulation remains below pre-pandemic levels (78.0% of baseline), reflecting a lasting shift toward digital lending.
    • Library income grew 24.8% from FY2019 to FY2024, outpacing usage recovery as federal relief (CARES, ARPA, IMLS ARP) bolstered budgets.
