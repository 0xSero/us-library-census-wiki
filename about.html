About this wiki

How this dataset was built, where the data comes from, and what's still missing.

Overview

This is a living, automated-crawl dataset of every public and private library in the United States, plus every federal, state, county, city, tribal, and special-district government website. Each record carries address, geocoordinates, website URL, funding information, contact details, review ratings, hours of operation, services offered, and area demographics. Every link is verified live, and the dataset is continuously enriched via a scheduled pipeline.

Known Gaps (honest accounting)

  • 14.5% of public library systems (1,339) have no website — almost all are tiny rural/bookmobile libraries with no online presence.
  • 54.1% of public libraries remain unrated — Google Places API daily quota limits accumulation; Gemini LLM fills in well-known libraries but correctly omits obscure branches.
  • 25.6% of gov sites are not live — many are genuinely expired/dead .gov domains (DNS won't resolve).
  • Special libraries (law, medical, theological, corporate) are underrepresented — only 75 added beyond the 3,695 academic libraries.
  • Cron automation is set up but not yet active — run bash automation/setup_cron.sh to enable continuous enrichment.

Update Frequency

The pipeline is designed to run every 30 minutes via cron (automation/run_pipeline.sh). Each run is idempotent and resumable — it picks up where the last run left off, using caches in data/_cache/. Google Places API quota resets daily at midnight PT, so ratings accumulate across runs. The wiki can be rebuilt at any time by re-running python3 wiki/build_wiki.py.

Key Historical Facts

  • The earliest American libraries were private collections of gentlemen, clergy, and colleges; Anglican parish libraries subsidized by the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel (founded 1701) appeared at colonial churches.
  • The Library Company of Philadelphia (1731, Benjamin Franklin and the Junto) was the first successful subscription library in America and hired Louis Timothee, the first librarian hired in America; it served virtually as the Library of Congress until the national capital was established in 1800.
  • The Darby Free Library (founded 1743 as the Darby Library Company by 29 Quakers) claims to be 'the oldest library in the United States in continuous service'; it was a subscription library until 1898 and celebrated its 275th anniversary in 2018.
  • The Redwood Library and Athenaeum (1747, Newport, RI) is the first purposely built library in the US and the oldest neo-Classical building in the country, in continuous use since its opening; it predates the Charleston Library Society (1748), New York Society Library (1754), and Boston Athenaeum (1807).
  • The Library of Congress was established April 24, 1800; British forces burned its original collection in August 1814; Congress bought Thomas Jefferson's 6,487 books for $23,950 in January 1815; and it gained copyright deposit rights in 1870.
  • Peterborough, New Hampshire, founded the first tax-supported free public library in the US (and the world) at town meeting on April 9, 1833; its success led New Hampshire to pass enabling legislation in 1849.
  • The Boston Public Library (statute 1848, established 1852, opened March 20, 1854) was the first large free municipal public library in the US; it is Massachusetts' Library for the Commonwealth and the third-largest public library in the US (~24 million items).
  • The American Library Association, founded October 6, 1876, in Philadelphia by 103 librarians (90 men, 13 women), is the oldest and largest library association in the world; Justin Winsor was its first president (1876-1885).
  • Melvil Dewey published the Dewey Decimal Classification in 1876 and opened the first library school in the world - the School of Library Economy at Columbia College - on January 5, 1887, with 20 students (17 women, admitted against the Regents' wishes).
  • Andrew Carnegie funded 2,509 libraries worldwide (1,689 in the US) between 1883 and 1929; $40 million built 1,670 public library buildings in 1,412 American communities, and by 1930 half of all US public libraries had been built by Carnegie.
  • Under segregation, Carnegie funded separate libraries for African Americans in the South (e.g., Houston Colored Carnegie Library; the Carnegie Library of Savannah, GA, opened 1914) rather than insisting on integration.
  • Women's clubs led the establishment of 75-80% of libraries in communities across the country during Carnegie's era, organizing local fundraising and lobbying.
  • The ALA Library War Service (1917-1920) raised $5 million, erected 36 camp libraries (with a $320,000 Carnegie Corporation grant), distributed 7-10 million books and magazines, and served 500+ locations including military hospitals in WWI; it led to library departments in the Army, Navy, Veterans' Association, and Merchant Marine, and the American Library in Paris (1918).
  • The first Library Bill of Rights was drafted by Forrest Spaulding and adopted by the ALA in 1939, defining modern librarianship's commitment to intellectual freedom; the ALA strengthened it in 1948 and amended it in 1961 to bar discrimination by race, religion, national origin, or political views.
  • The National Library Service for the Blind (Pratt-Smoot Act, began July 1, 1931) is the federal library service for blind and print-disabled Americans; it was amended to add sound recordings/talking books (1934), blind children (1952), music materials (1962), and physical impairments (1966).

    Key Findings

    • COVID-19 caused the sharpest one-year drop in US public library usage on record: visits fell 41.6% (FY2019 -> FY2020) and circulation fell 25.0%.
    • Despite the FY2020 trough, library income actually rose 2.1% in FY2020 thanks to pre-pandemic budgets and federal relief.
    • By FY2024, visits had recovered to 69.3% of baseline and programs to 85.8% -- meaningful but incomplete recovery.
    • Circulation remains below pre-pandemic levels (78.0% of baseline), reflecting a lasting shift toward digital lending.
    • Library income grew 24.8% from FY2019 to FY2024, outpacing usage recovery as federal relief (CARES, ARPA, IMLS ARP) bolstered budgets.

    Library District Laws

    Library districts are special-purpose governmental units with independent taxing authority. They represent one of the most powerful funding mechanisms for libraries in the U.S.

    • Independent library district (elected board, taxing authority): Special-purpose political subdivision with elected trustees and voter-approved property-tax authority; the strongest autonomous model.
    • County/parish library district (appointed board, taxing authority): County- or parish-wide library with appointed board and voter-approved millage/levy.
    • Regional library system (appointed board, no independent taxing): Multicounty cooperative funded by member governments and state aid; no separate taxing district.
    • Special district (varied): Authorization exists for special/library districts, but funding mechanisms and autonomy vary widely.

    Key Findings

    • All 50 states and DC have library enabling legislation authorizing the creation of public libraries, but only a subset authorize independent library districts with taxing authority; Colorado, Illinois, Michigan, New York, Oregon, and Washington are leading district states.
    • Library governance divides between appointed boards (the majority) and elected boards (concentrated in library-district states and New England town-meeting states); board size is most commonly 5, 7, or 9 members with staggered three-year terms, though Ohio's seven-year terms are an outlier.
    • Every state has an open meetings law applying to library boards, requiring notice, open deliberation, and minutes, with executive sessions limited to enumerated topics such as personnel and litigation.
    • Every state has a public records / FOI law applying to libraries, but patron circulation and registration records are universally carved out by library-privacy statutes, creating a near-nationwide wall between administrative transparency and reader privacy.
    • Approximately 50 states have some form of library record confidentiality; a handful historically relied on general privacy law or attorney-general opinion before enacting library-specific provisions.
    • Roughly half of states require statewide public librarian certification, typically population-tiered and tied to ALA-accredited MLIS for the largest jurisdictions; states without mandatory certification still commonly require the MLIS at the employer level.
    • The first sale doctrine (Bobbs-Merrill v. Straus, 1908; codified at 17 U.S.C. § 109) is the legal foundation of physical library lending, while § 108 provides libraries and archives reproduction exemptions for preservation, replacement, and interlibrary loan.
    • Digital lending remains constrained: Capitol Records v. ReDigi (2018) rejected a digital first sale doctrine, so libraries rely on licensed e-book platforms rather than § 109 for digital circulation.

      Key Findings

      • U.S. library history begins with private, church, and bequest collections; Captain Robert Keayne's 1656 Boston book bequest is the earliest colonial precursor.
      • Benjamin Franklin's Library Company of Philadelphia (1731) was the first successful subscription library in the colonies and the model for the 'library company' form nationwide.
      • The transition to tax-supported public libraries culminated in the Peterborough, New Hampshire town library (1833, first tax-supported public library) and the Boston Public Library (1848 founded / 1854 opened, first large municipally supported free public library).
      • Boston Public Library achieved multiple U.S. 'firsts': first branch (East Boston, 1870), first to open free to the public (1854), and first dedicated children's room (1895).
      • Andrew Carnegie's library program funded 1,419 public grants producing 1,687 public library buildings plus 109 academic libraries in the United States between 1886 and 1923, totaling about $45.86 million (the Wikipedia 'Totals' row reports $45,865,440.10; the sum of the per-state rows parsed here is $45,864,940.10, a $500 difference from sub-row handling in the source table). Alaska and Delaware received no Carnegie libraries; Pennsylvania received Carnegie's first U.S. grant in 1886.
      • The states receiving the most Carnegie funding were New York ($6,697,149), Pennsylvania ($5,610,587), Ohio ($3,239,928.64), California ($2,836,987), and Indiana ($2,588,664.38). Indiana received the most public grants (156).
      • Melvil Dewey founded the ALA (1876), invented the Dewey Decimal Classification (1876), and established the first U.S. library school (1887); Charles Ammi Cutter produced America's first public card catalog and shaped modern classification.
      • Mary Lemist Titcomb launched the first U.S. bookwagon at Washington County Free Library, Hagerstown, Maryland, in 1905.

      Summer Reading Programs

      Summer reading is the single most universal public-library program in the United States. Two national collaboratives supply the artwork, manuals, incentives, and slogans that the majority of public libraries use: the Collaborative Summer Library Program (CSLP), a grassroots consortium, and iREAD, a program of the Illinois Library Association (ILA). Summer reading's documented purpose is to prevent the 'summer slide' in reading achievement, especially among children from low-income households who lack access to books when school is not in session.

      Collaborative Summer Library Program (CSLP) — Founded 1987. A member-funded consortium. Member states/territories pay dues and, through shared resources and collaborative committees, make it easier and more affordable for libraries to host high-quality summer reading programs. Any profit from the sale of materials is reinvested into the organization.

      iREAD (Illinois Library Association) — Supplies high-quality, low-cost reading-program resources and products (artwork, manuals, incentives) to libraries by purchase. Originally an Illinois program ('I Read') that expanded nationally; libraries outside Illinois may buy iREAD materials.

      Impact Studies

      • The Dominican Study: Public Library Summer Reading Programs Close the Reading Gap (, 2010): Students who participated in public-library summer reading programs scored higher on reading-achievement tests than non-participants and did not experience the 'summer slide'; the effect was strongest for students in the lowest socioeconomic groups. The study is the most widely cited evidence base for the academic value of library summer reading.
      • Reading Is Fundamental (RIF) / library access research: Children's access to self-selected books over the summer is associated with measurable gains in reading proficiency; library summer-reading programs are a principal mechanism for delivering that access at scale.

      Early Literacy Programs

      Early literacy -- the pre-reading skills children develop from birth to age five -- is a core mission of U.S. public libraries. Programming centers on storytimes and on educating parents and caregivers to be their child's first teacher. The dominant national framework is Every Child Ready to Read (ECRR), complemented by grassroots challenges such as 1000 Books Before Kindergarten and 'Born to Read' newborn programs.

      Every Child Ready to Read (ECRR) @ your library — A parent-education initiative. PLA and ALSC concluded that public libraries could have an even greater impact on early literacy by educating parents and caregivers (rather than only modeling skills in storytimes). Teaching caregivers how to support early-literacy development multiplies the effect of library efforts (ECRR site).

      1000 Books Before Kindergarten — A nationwide reading challenge: read a book (any book, repeats count) to a newborn, infant, and/or toddler, with the goal of 1,000 books before the child starts kindergarten. The 1000 Books Foundation enables libraries, schools, churches, and other nonprofit entities to host the challenge locally; contributions are tax-deductible (1000booksbeforekindergarten.org).
